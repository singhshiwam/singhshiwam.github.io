Surface layer processes in weather prediction models over mountains
Centre of Excellence in Disaster Mitigation and Management, IIT Roorkee

I study the exchange of heat and momentum between the ground and the air just above it. Weather models handle this reasonably well over flat land. Over mountains, slope, stability and terrain-driven flow break the assumptions those schemes were built on. I use tower measurements from the Himalaya to test where the standard formulations fail, and to work out what performs better in complex terrain.

What I work on

How surface-atmosphere exchange is represented in numerical weather prediction models over complex terrain, and where those representations fail.

research statement

Surface layer parameterization

Monin-Obukhov similarity theory provides the flux calculation in nearly every operational model. It assumes horizontal homogeneity, flat terrain and constant flux with height, none of which hold over a mountain. I compare how different stability functions perform against measured fluxes, particularly under stable stratification at night, and quantify the error that results.

Turbulence measurements

The evaluation depends on direct measurement. I work with high-frequency eddy covariance data from sonic anemometers sampling at 20 Hz and above, and compute turbulent heat and momentum fluxes from it. Despiking, quality control, coordinate rotation and sorting the record by stability regime all come before anything is compared against the model.

High-resolution modelling

WRF-ARW is the model I use most, with the urban canopy model and the chemistry module where the problem needs them, and RegCM for longer regional integrations. At kilometre resolution over the Himalaya the results are sensitive to terrain smoothing, land-use classification and the choice of boundary layer scheme, so much of the work is systematic sensitivity testing.

Link to disaster forecasting

An error in the simulated surface energy balance carries into the simulated convection, which matters for forecasting cloudbursts and the rainfall that triggers landslides in the Himalaya. I contribute the atmospheric modelling to group work of this kind, including a study of the July 2024 Wayanad landslide and an impact-based heat stress warning framework for a coastal city.

Mathematics background

I came to atmospheric science from pure mathematics, with a B.Sc. and M.Sc. in the subject and master's projects on the Riemann-Roch theorem for function fields and on arcs in finite projective spaces. It is useful for working inside the numerics rather than treating the model as a black box: the schemes are differential equations with closure assumptions, and both can be checked directly.
